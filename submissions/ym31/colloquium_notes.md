# Colloquium Notes 9/8

- Main question: where are all the black holes in the Milky Way? Should be ~10^8 of them
  based on how many O stars there are, we only know about 100
- Most known ones are X-ray binaries
- Gaia measures star positions super precisely. If a star orbits a dark object it wobbles,
  fit the orbit and Kepler's laws give the companion mass. That's the whole method
- DR3 only used 3 years of data and already found way more binary orbits than before
- 2 black holes confirmed from DR3, BH1 and BH2, both ~9 solar masses around sun-like stars
- BH3 came out early from DR4, 33 solar masses, very metal poor, in a stellar stream
- Formation problem: the star that became the black hole should have expanded into a red
  supergiant and swallowed the companion before it ever became a black hole. So how does
  the companion survive? Ideas were it never expanded, it was a triple and the other massive
  star stopped it from expanding, or it got the companion later through an exchange in a
  cluster. He said there are more models than black holes at this point
- The companion star gives you the metallicity. Metal poor means weaker winds means
  bigger black hole. He fit a line to 3 points and said not to trust it
- The 3 Gaia black holes are the closest black holes known so these quiet ones are
  probably common
- Also a bunch of neutron star candidates around 1.3-2 solar masses. Nothing between 2 and
  5, mass gap
- Metal poor neutron star companions have extra lithium, unknown why
- Kicks: some black holes get big kicks (>100 km/s) but V404 Cyg has a distant third star
  still bound to it so that one got almost no kick
- DR4 in December, ~10x more binaries
- Q&A: someone asked if the 1.3 solar mass objects could be heavy white dwarfs instead of
  neutron stars. He said you can't rule it out for any single one
